\paragraph{Calibration and verbalized confidence.}
Language models can estimate whether their own answers are correct, for example by stating the probability that a
proposed answer is true~\citep{kadavath2022know}.
For models fine-tuned with human feedback, confidences stated in words are often better calibrated than the model's
token probabilities~\citep{tian2023justask}, but stated confidences tend to be overconfident, and consistency among
several sampled answers can help~\citep{xiong2024express}.
Language and vision-language models are mostly overconfident when they verbalize uncertainty, and vision-language
models are poorly calibrated when they state a mean and standard deviation or an interval for a numerical
answer~\citep{groot2024overconfidence}.
Intervals that models construct around their own numerical estimates cover the truth far less often than their
nominal level~\citep{epstein2025fermieval}, including in quantitative forecasting~\citep{qin2026quantsight}.
These works evaluate pooled calibration against outcomes; because the right uncertainty of each question is unknown,
they cannot test whether a stated interval tracks it.
Our generator knows each problem's $\sigstar$, so we can ask that per problem, and our pair sets ask whether $\sigma$
moves when only the source of noise moves.

\paragraph{Reinforcement learning with proper scoring rules.}
RLCR adds a Brier score on a stated confidence to a binary correctness reward, and proves that a bounded proper
scoring rule in the reward yields answers that are both accurate and calibrated~\citep{damani2026rlcr}.
Rewarding Doubt trains a stated confidence on factual questions with the logarithmic scoring
rule~\citep{baniharouni2026doubt}.
Both target the probability that an answer is correct, not a distribution over a continuous quantity.
Confidence-aware RL is limited by the model's confidence prior before RL, which can concentrate on a few high values and
persist through training~\citep{wang2026pitfalls}; under sequence-level credit we observe a related failure, a stated
$\sigma$ collapsing towards zero (Section~\ref{sec:act1}).
For LLM regression, DAR scores the empirical distribution of several sampled predictions and credits each prediction
by its leave-one-out contribution~\citep{park2026dar}.
A CRPS-type reward is therefore not our contribution: in DAR the uncertainty is the spread of rollouts, whereas we
train a single stated $\sigma$ and score it against a known $\sigstar$.

\paragraph{Sampling, self-verification and executable computation.}
Self-consistency samples several reasoning paths and takes the most consistent answer~\citep{wang2023selfconsistency};
we use the agreement among samples as a correctness signal and report its cost beside its discrimination.
Without external feedback, models struggle to correct their own reasoning, and their performance can even degrade
when they try~\citep{huang2024selfcorrect}; our self-verification probe is consistent with this, since the model
accepts almost all of its own answers.
Program-aided reasoning delegates the computation to an interpreter~\citep{gao2023pal,chen2023pot}.
Our MC-B applies that idea to uncertainty: the model writes the problem's program and the program is run on perturbed
inputs.
These works limit MC-B's novelty, and MC-B failed (Section~\ref{sec:act3}).

\paragraph{Uncertainty propagation and proper scoring.}
Our $\sigstar$ follows the metrological practice of propagating distributions through a measurement model by Monte
Carlo~\citep{jcgm101}, and the root-sum-square heuristic is the first-order rule for a product of first powers.
Our reward and evaluation score, the CRPS in log space, is strictly proper for log-normal beliefs in the sense of
Gneiting and Raftery~\citep{gneiting2007strictly}, so an honest $\sigma$ is optimal in expectation; whether optimization finds it
is the question of Section~\ref{sec:act1}.
